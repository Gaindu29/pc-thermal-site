01 — What Is GPU Overclocking?

GPU overclocking is the process of pushing your graphics card's core clock and memory clock speeds beyond their factory defaults. Modern GPUs boost dynamically, meaning they already run faster than their base clock in real-time — overclocking extends this ceiling further.

The two primary levers are:

  • Core Clock (GPU clock): Controls how fast the shader processors, rasterizers, and compute units run. A higher core clock directly increases frame rates in GPU-bound scenarios.
  • Memory Clock (VRAM clock): Controls the speed of the video memory. Faster memory increases bandwidth, which matters significantly at higher resolutions (1440p, 4K) and in bandwidth-limited games and applications.

A well-executed overclock typically yields 5–15% more performance with no financial cost — just time and some patience. On a high-end GPU, that can translate to 10–20 extra frames per second in demanding titles.

⚠️ Important: Overclocking is safe when done incrementally and tested properly. However, it can void warranties on some cards and carries a small risk of instability (crashes, black screens) if pushed too far. This guide focuses on conservative, daily-driver overclocks — not record-chasing extreme OC.

03 — Safe Overclocking Temperature Targets

The most important rule in GPU overclocking: the goal is performance, not heat. If your overclock produces temperatures that are uncomfortably close to TJ Max, it's not a good overclock — it's a throttling overclock.

GDDR6X Memory Junction Temperature: On cards with GDDR6X VRAM (RTX 3080, 3080 Ti, 3090, 3090 Ti, 4080, 4090), HWiNFO64 will show a "GPU Memory Junction Temperature" sensor. This can run 20–30°C above the core temperature. Keep it below 100°C during overclocked loads. This is the primary limiting factor for memory overclocks on these cards.

04 — Step-by-Step Overclocking Process

1

Baseline Your GPU

Before touching any settings, run a benchmark (3DMark TimeSpy) and record the score, peak GPU temperature, and peak power draw. This is your baseline. Open HWiNFO64 → Sensors to monitor temps in real time during the run.

2

Increase Power Limit First

In MSI Afterburner, raise the Power Limit slider to its maximum (usually +10 to +25% depending on card). This alone can free up several MHz of boost clock that the card was previously throttling to stay within TDP. Run your benchmark again — some cards gain 2–5% here with zero other changes.

3

Set Fan Curve

Before any clock changes, set a more aggressive fan curve. In Afterburner, click the Fan icon and enable "Define your own fan profile." A good starting curve: reach 70% fan speed at 70°C, 85% at 80°C. This ensures the card can sustain higher clocks without thermal throttling.

4

Overclock Core Clock — Incrementally

Start with a +100 MHz core clock offset in Afterburner. Click Apply, then run Superposition for 10 minutes or play a demanding game for 20–30 minutes. If stable, increase by +25 MHz and repeat. Keep going until you hit a crash, artifact (visual glitch), or driver timeout. Then back off by 25–50 MHz — that's your stable core overclock.

5

Overclock Memory Clock — Separately

With core stable, reset core to stock and start pushing memory. Increase in +100 MHz steps for GDDR6X or +200 MHz steps for GDDR6. Artifacts often appear as texture flickering or coloured pixels before a crash — these are your warning signs. Back off 100–200 MHz from the point of artifacts.

6

Combine and Final Stability Test

Apply your stable core + memory overclock together with the raised power limit. Run a 30-minute Superposition stress test at maximum settings, then run 3DMark TimeSpy twice to confirm the score is repeatable. Check that peak temperatures stayed within safe targets. If stable through all of this, your overclock is solid.

7

Save the Profile

In MSI Afterburner, save your final settings to a profile slot (1–5 in the interface). Enable "Apply overclocking at system startup" so the OC loads automatically. Use the hardware buttons on the Afterburner interface or a keyboard shortcut to toggle profiles on/off.

05 — Stress Testing Your Overclock

An overclock that crashes once in 100 hours isn't stable — it will crash at the worst time. Proper stress testing gives confidence before relying on an OC daily.

TestDurationWhat It CatchesSeverity
Superposition (1080p Extreme) 20–30 min Core and memory instability, sustained temps High
3DMark TimeSpy Loop 5 runs Score consistency, crash under repeated load Medium
FurMark (burn-in) 10–15 min Extreme thermal stress — unrealistically harsh Very High
In-game (demanding title) 45–60 min Real-world stability in actual usage patterns Realistic
Recommended order: Superposition → 3DMark loop → 45 minutes of your most GPU-intensive game. If you pass all three, the OC is solid for daily use.

06 — Signs of an Unstable Overclock

  • Driver timeout / black screen recovery — Windows "Display driver stopped responding and has recovered" — your most common instability signal. Reduce core or memory by 25–50 MHz.
  • Texture artifacts / pixel flickering — coloured dots, striped patterns, or texture errors. Usually a sign of memory OC being too high. Back off memory immediately.
  • Hard crash / BSOD — blue screen or complete system freeze. Usually indicates too aggressive a core clock or power limit. Reduce core offset by 50 MHz.
  • Lower benchmark scores under OC — if your score drops compared to stock, the GPU is likely thermal throttling under the overclock. Check peak temperatures in HWiNFO64.
  • Random game crashes that weren't happening before — the most common user-reported sign. Don't ignore these; track when they started relative to OC changes.
GDDR6X Memory Warning: On RTX 3080, 3090, 4080, and 4090, excessive memory overclocking causes VRAM temperatures to spike. If Memory Junction Temperature (visible in HWiNFO64) exceeds 100°C, reduce the memory offset — thermal damage to GDDR6X chips is possible with sustained operation above this threshold.

Undervolting: The Hidden Win

Most overclock guides focus purely on pushing clocks up — but undervolting is often a better strategy, especially on power-limited or thermally constrained builds.

Undervolting reduces the voltage supplied to the GPU while maintaining the same (or even higher) clock speeds. This results in:

  • Lower temperatures (5–15°C reduction is common)
  • Lower noise levels from reduced fan demand
  • Maintained or improved performance (no thermal throttle)
  • Longer component lifespan

On NVIDIA GPUs, open MSI Afterburner's Voltage/Frequency curve editor with Ctrl + F. Find the highest frequency point your card reaches and drag all points to the right of it downward. Then flatten the curve at your target voltage (typically 925–975 mV for RTX 30 series, 1050–1100 mV for RTX 40 series).

On AMD GPUs, AMD Adrenalin's Performance → Tuning section has a built-in manual voltage/frequency slider that achieves the same result.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does overclocking reduce GPU lifespan?

A conservative overclock within safe temperature limits has a negligible effect on GPU lifespan. Modern GPUs have thermal and voltage protections that prevent truly damaging conditions under normal overclocking. The greater lifespan risk comes from sustained high temperatures — which is why monitoring and maintaining safe temps while overclocking is essential.

Does overclocking void my GPU warranty?

This varies by manufacturer. EVGA had a warranty-friendly OC policy before exiting the GPU market. Most other AIBs (ASUS, Gigabyte, MSI, Sapphire, ZOTAC) technically allow overclocking but reserve the right to deny warranty claims for damage deemed caused by OC. In practice, if a GPU fails under a mild overclock with no visible signs of damage, most RMA processes proceed normally. Extreme overclocking (unlocked BIOS, excessive voltage) is higher risk for warranty denial.

Why does my score go down when I overclock?

If benchmark scores decrease after applying an overclock, the GPU is almost certainly thermal throttling — running too hot to sustain the boosted clocks. The overclock adds more heat than the cooler can dissipate, causing the GPU to reduce clocks below even stock performance. Fix: lower the overclock, improve case airflow, or adjust the fan curve to be more aggressive.

What is the silicon lottery?

GPUs (and CPUs) are manufactured with slight variations in the quality of each individual chip. Some chips, even from the same production batch, can achieve significantly higher stable clocks at the same voltage — these are "high lottery" chips. Others max out at lower clocks. This is why overclocking results can vary by 100 MHz or more between two identical GPU models. The recommended settings in this guide represent safe conservative baselines that the vast majority of chips should achieve without issue.
